Overview

In this assignment I built a real-time cloth simulator on top of a mass-spring model. The cloth is a 50 × 50 grid of point masses connected by structural, shearing and bending springs. I implemented the grid construction, spring forces and Verlet integration, collisions against spheres and planes, and a spatial-hash self-collision pass. The second half of the assignment moves the lighting to the GPU: I wrote GLSL shaders for diffuse and Blinn-Phong shading, texture mapping, bump mapping, displacement mapping, environment-mapped reflections, and a custom iridescent fabric shader.

Challenges

The main simulation challenge was keeping self-collision stable. Pushing every nearby pair apart by the full overlap over-corrects when several point masses share a hash cell, so I average the correction over the neighbours and divide it by the number of simulation substeps; that keeps the cloth from exploding while still separating the layers.

On the shader side, the tricky part was constructing a consistent tangent-space frame. I build the bitangent as cross(normal, tangent), transform the local normal (-dU, -dV, 1) through the TBN matrix, and normalize the result. Using the same height map in the bump and displacement shaders made it easier to compare the two approaches.

Part 1: Masses and Springs

Cloth::buildGrid first creates the point masses in row-major (x-axis) order so that the renderer and the spring indices agree. For a HORIZONTAL cloth I set y = 1 and vary x and z; for a VERTICAL cloth I vary x and y and add a random z offset in [-1/1000, 1/1000] to break the symmetry. A mass is pinned if its (x, y) index appears in the scene's pinned list. Only after every mass exists do I create springs, because each spring stores pointers into point_masses.

Structural springs connect a mass to its left and upper neighbours, shearing springs connect the two upper diagonals, and bending springs connect masses two cells to the left and two cells above. For the 50 × 50 scenes this produces 2,500 masses and 14,502 springs (4,900 structural, 4,802 shearing, 4,800 bending).

With shearing disabled the grid still holds together horizontally and vertically, but the square cells can shear freely, so the wireframe looks like a loose net. With only shearing enabled the diagonals are visible but there are no edge-aligned structural springs. Turning all three on gives the familiar triangulated cloth: squares plus both diagonals, with the bending springs spanning every other cell.

Part 2: Simulation via Numerical Integration

Cloth::simulate advances the system by dt = 1 / (frames_per_sec * simulation_steps) = 1 / (90 * 30) ≈ 3.7e-4 s. The mass of one point mass is width * height * density / (num_width_points * num_height_points). Every frame I reset the force on each mass to mass * external_acceleration (gravity), then add the spring force F = ks (|d| - rest_length) d / |d| to both endpoints. Bending springs use 0.2 ks, which makes the cloth resist folding without making it rigid.

I integrate with Verlet: xt+1 = xt + (1 - damping/100)(xt - xt-1) + a dt2. Pinned masses are skipped. After integration I run self-collision, collide every mass with the scene's spheres and planes, and finally enforce the Provot constraint: no spring may stretch beyond 110% of its rest length. The correction is split between the two endpoints, or applied entirely to the free endpoint when the other one is pinned.

Spring constant

A low ks makes the cloth soft and stretchy: it sags well below its rest length, the pinned corners carry most of the weight, and the surface develops deep folds. A high ks makes it stiff: it keeps its original size, hangs almost straight down and barely stretches. In the 400-frame rest captures the lowest point is about y = -0.149 for ks = 100, y = -0.083 for ks = 5000, and y = -0.022 for ks = 50000.

Density

Density changes the mass, and therefore changes how strongly gravity competes with the springs. At density = 15 the cloth is light relative to its spring stiffness, so it behaves like a stiff cloth and barely sags. At density = 1500 gravity dominates, the cloth stretches and folds much more, and the lowest point drops to about y = -0.143. This is consistent with the equations: the spring acceleration scales as ks / mass, so lowering density has roughly the same effect as raising ks.

Damping

Damping removes velocity each step. With damping = 0 the cloth keeps swinging and oscillating around its rest shape even after 400 frames; the motion is still clearly visible. With damping = 90 almost all velocity is removed every step, so the cloth settles immediately and stays close to its starting horizontal position (the lowest point is still about y = 0.982 after 400 frames). The default damping = 0.2 is a good middle ground: the cloth falls, overshoots slightly and comes to rest.

Part 3: Handling Collisions with Other Objects

For a sphere I compute d = p - origin. If |d| >= radius the mass is outside and nothing happens. Otherwise the closest point on the sphere is origin + radius * d / |d|. I correct the mass from its previous position toward that tangent point, scaled by (1 - friction), so friction controls how much of the correction survives: 

tangent = origin + radius * normalize(p - origin);
p = last_position + (1 - friction) * (tangent - last_position);

For a plane I use the signed distances of the current and previous positions. If their signs differ, the mass crossed the plane during this step. I project the current position onto the plane, move it back to the side the mass came from by SURFACE_OFFSET, and again blend from last_position with friction. This prevents the cloth from sticking to or tunnelling through the plane.

With ks = 500 the cloth is soft, so it stretches over the sphere and the corners hang far down the sides; the lowest point is about y = -0.262. The default ks = 5000 still drapes but holds its shape better, reaching about y = -0.231. With ks = 50000 the cloth is stiff and behaves more like a shell resting on the sphere; it folds less and stays higher (y ≈ -0.197).

Part 4: Handling Self-Collisions

Self-collision uses a spatial hash grid. Each cell is three point-mass spacings wide in x and y, and three times the larger spacing in z; the cell coordinates are folded into a single float key. Every step I rebuild the map and, for each mass, compare it only with the other masses in the same cell. If another mass is closer than 2 * thickness, I accumulate the vector needed to separate the pair and add the average correction divided by simulation_steps: 

for each neighbour n within 2 * thickness:
    correction += normalize(p - n) * (2 * thickness - |p - n|);
p += correction / num_corrections / simulation_steps;

Dividing by the number of simulation steps keeps the correction stable when several substeps run per frame. The hash makes the pass roughly linear in the number of masses instead of comparing all pairs.

The cloth starts vertical, falls onto itself and the plane, and the layers stay separated instead of passing through each other. Without self-collision the folds would intersect and the cloth would collapse into a thin sheet. The early frames show the first contact between layers; by the late frames the stack is compressed but still has visible thickness.

At low density the cloth is light and behaves stiffly: it unfolds more and the stack is thinner, much like the high-ks case. At high density gravity dominates, so the cloth falls faster, compresses the lower layers more and produces a thicker, more crumpled pile. Lowering ks makes the cloth softer: it collapses flatter against the plane and the layers press together, while raising ks makes it hold its folds and stack higher (the peak is about y = 0.097 at ks = 50000 versus y = 0.058 at ks = 500).

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program is a pair of small GPU programs that replace fixed-function stages of the graphics pipeline. For the sphere-only figures below I used a small demo scene in which a 1 × 1 cloth is pinned above the sphere, so the sphere itself is never hidden by the draped cloth. The vertex shader runs once per vertex: it transforms the position into clip space with the model and view-projection matrices and passes world-space position, normal, tangent and UV coordinates to the next stage. The rasterizer interpolates those values across each triangle, and the fragment shader runs once per covered pixel. The fragment shader receives the interpolated values plus uniforms such as the camera position, light position/intensity, material color and textures, and writes the final RGBA color. Because thousands of vertices and fragments run in parallel, this is what makes real-time lighting possible.

Blinn-Phong shading

Blinn-Phong combines three terms. Ambient is a constant base color. Diffuse is kd (I / r2) max(0, n · l), where l points from the surface to the light. Specular uses the half vector h = normalize(l + v) and is ks (I / r2) max(0, n · h)p; the exponent p = 64 controls how tight the highlight is. Using the half vector instead of the reflected view vector makes the highlight cheaper and keeps it stable at grazing angles. All three terms use inverse-square distance falloff 1/r2.

Texture mapping

The texture shader samples u_texture_1 at the interpolated UV and writes that color directly. I replaced the default texture with a custom generated pattern and use it on both the cloth and the sphere.

Bump mapping and displacement mapping

Both shaders use the same grayscale height map (the custom texture_3, not the default texture_2). For bump mapping I take finite differences of the height in u and v, scale them by the height and normal scaling uniforms, and form a local normal (-dU, -dV, 1). The tangent, bitangent and normal vectors define the TBN matrix, so normalize(TBN * n_local) is the perturbed world-space normal used for the Blinn-Phong terms. Displacement mapping additionally moves each vertex along its normal by height * height_scaling in the vertex shader, and the fragment shader rebuilds the normal the same way.

Bump mapping only changes the shading normal, so the silhouette is still the original sphere mesh. At -o 16 -a 16 the coarse triangles are visible as faceted patches, while at -o 128 -a 128 the smooth interpolation makes the bumps look like a continuous surface. Displacement actually moves the vertices, so at low resolution the sphere becomes a visibly faceted, spiky mesh; increasing the resolution resolves the height field into fine detail. Bump is much cheaper and is enough when the height variations are small, while displacement gives real parallax and a correct silhouette at the cost of needing a dense mesh.

Environment-mapped reflections

The mirror shader computes the outgoing eye direction wo = normalize(v_position - u_cam_pos), reflects it around the surface normal with reflect(wo, n), and samples the cubemap along the reflected direction. This makes the cloth and sphere pick up the surrounding environment instead of a single point light.

Custom shader

My custom shader models a thin iridescent fabric. The hue cycles with the viewing angle and with the UV coordinates, producing a color shift similar to a thin-film interference effect. A procedural sin-based warp/weft pattern adds a woven micro-structure. On top of that I apply Blinn-Phong shading, and a Fresnel-weighted cubemap reflection makes the sheen stronger at grazing angles. The result is a cloth that changes color as it folds and catches the environment at its edges.
